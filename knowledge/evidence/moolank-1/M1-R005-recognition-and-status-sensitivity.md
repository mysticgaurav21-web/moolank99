## Research question

Do traditional Birth Number / Moolank 1 sources consistently support a preference for recognition, visibility or respect, and how should this be separated from confidence, self-esteem, ambition, dominance, entitlement, social comparison and dependence on praise?

## Working definition

Recognition and status sensitivity describe how strongly acknowledgment, fair credit, visibility, rank, respect and public evaluation are noticed, and how those signals influence motivation, self-presentation or cooperation.

Healthy recognition sensitivity values accurate acknowledgment and proportionate credit without making praise, rank or visibility the basis of personal worth. It does not automatically mean entitlement, superiority, praise dependence, dominance, narcissism or fragile self-esteem.

## Psychology bridge

Psychology is used only to define constructs and safe boundaries. It does **not** validate a relationship between birth number and recognition sensitivity.

### Recognition versus praise

Recognition can include accurate acknowledgment, credit, respect, visibility or confirmation that contribution was noticed. Praise is an explicitly positive evaluation. A person may value fair credit without requiring admiration.

### Informational versus controlling praise

Self-determination research distinguishes feedback that communicates useful competence information from praise that pressures behaviour or makes approval conditional. Informational acknowledgment can support competence; controlling praise can shift attention toward approval management.

Relevant references:

- Deci and Ryan, **Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior** (1985).
- Deci, Koestner and Ryan, meta-analytic work on rewards and intrinsic motivation.

### Contingent self-worth

Contingent self-worth occurs when personal value rises or falls heavily with approval, success, comparison or status outcomes. It is distinct from appreciating recognition.

Relevant reference:

- Crocker and Wolfe, **“Contingencies of Self-Worth”** (2001).

### Social comparison

Social comparison concerns evaluating opinions, abilities or standing relative to others. It can support learning, but may also intensify visibility competition, resentment or status monitoring when comparison becomes identity-relevant.

Relevant reference:

- Festinger, **“A Theory of Social Comparison Processes”** (1954).

### Status threat

Status threat refers to perceived loss of rank, respect, influence or relative standing. Possible responses are contextual and can include competition, withdrawal, image protection or intensified credit claims. These are general boundary constructs, not Moolank-specific facts.

## Evidence synthesis

### Traditional core retained

Moolank 1 tradition may support a preference for visible contribution, respectful acknowledgment and fair credit, especially in roles involving responsibility, initiative or leadership.

### Balanced expression

- values accurate acknowledgment rather than empty praise;
- appreciates proportionate credit for contribution;
- recognizes others without feeling diminished;
- separates respect from automatic agreement;
- remains motivated when recognition is delayed;
- communicates credit expectations directly;
- accepts shared ownership where work is collaborative;
- contributes privately when the outcome matters;
- does not equate low visibility with low worth;
- uses recognition as feedback rather than identity proof;
- distinguishes title or rank from actual contribution;
- can celebrate another person’s success without treating it as personal loss.

### Conditional shadow expression

The shadow is not universal. It becomes more plausible when recognition needs combine with public comparison, unclear ownership, unequal credit, status competition, repeated invisibility, insecurity, recent success or fear of replacement.

Possible patterns:

- feels overlooked when contribution is not publicly named;
- interprets disagreement as disrespect;
- competes for visibility beyond task needs;
- takes disproportionate credit for shared outcomes;
- withholds cooperation when acknowledgment seems absent;
- resents another person receiving praise;
- uses title, rank or authority to protect standing;
- prioritizes visible work over necessary low-visibility work;
- depends on applause to sustain effort;
- treats reduced recognition as proof of reduced worth;
- exaggerates contribution when ownership is ambiguous;
- minimizes others’ contribution to preserve relative status.

## Safe conditional triggers

- contribution omitted from public credit;
- unclear ownership or role attribution;
- another person receiving praise for shared work;
- demotion, title loss or reduced visibility;
- public disagreement interpreted as status loss;
- repeated invisible or support work;
- comparison with a highly visible peer;
- leadership transition or fear of replacement;
- inconsistent recognition standards;
- praise-heavy environments that make approval feel necessary.

These are possible contexts, not deterministic causes.

## Protective factors

- clear ownership and credit rules before work begins;
- specific contribution-based acknowledgment;
- private and public recognition used appropriately;
- shared-credit norms for collaborative outcomes;
- separating personal worth from visibility and rank;
- outcome and learning goals alongside recognition goals;
- direct conversation before assuming disrespect;
- regular review of invisible and support work;
- transparent promotion, title and reward criteria;
- rotating visible opportunities;
- recognition of others as a leadership responsibility;
- continuing meaningful effort when praise is delayed.

## Construct boundaries

### Recognition sensitivity vs confidence

Confidence concerns perceived capability. Recognition sensitivity concerns how acknowledgment and public evaluation affect motivation or self-presentation. Confidence can remain stable without praise.

### Recognition sensitivity vs self-esteem

Self-esteem concerns general self-worth. Valuing fair credit does not prove fragile self-esteem. Treating recognition as proof of worth would indicate contingent self-worth, which remains separate.

### Recognition sensitivity vs ambition

Ambition concerns desired achievement or advancement. Recognition may accompany ambition but does not define it.

### Recognition sensitivity vs dominance

Dominance concerns control, rank or influence over others. Seeking fair credit does not justify controlling others or suppressing dissent.

### Recognition sensitivity vs entitlement

Entitlement assumes special treatment regardless of contribution, agreement or context. Healthy recognition sensitivity asks for proportionate credit and respect.

### Recognition sensitivity vs praise dependence

Praise dependence means motivation or self-worth relies heavily on repeated approval. Recognition sensitivity may exist without dependence when meaningful effort continues despite delayed recognition.

### Recognition sensitivity vs social comparison

Social comparison is the process of evaluating oneself relative to others. Recognition sensitivity concerns response to acknowledgment and standing. Comparison may trigger it but is not identical to it.

### Recognition sensitivity vs leadership

Leadership concerns influence, coordination and shared-outcome responsibility. Effective leadership requires sharing credit, recognizing invisible work and tolerating dissent.

Canonical-safe provisional core:
